Physics

What's inside, and how it was matched

This toy is built around a mask-ROM CMOS "sound effect" chip. In toys like this the die is usually a black epoxy blob (chip-on-board) with no part number. The family it belongs to is documented in Holtek's HT2884 "8-Sound Generator" datasheet: an RC oscillator (typically 128 kHz with a 180 kΩ resistor), a programmable tone divider, a noise (LFSR) generator, a small sequencer ROM, and a 1-bit push-pull output (OUT / OUT̄) wired straight across an 8 Ω speaker. The chip has no DAC and no volume envelope: every sound is a sequence of square-wave or noise segments.

The numbers come from the clip that went round on Threads. Every tone in it sits at f = 32 335 Hz / N with an integer divider N, and neighbouring steps differ in period by exactly 1/32 335 s. That points to an oscillator at about 129 kHz divided by 4, which agrees with the datasheet's 128 kHz. The tones are 1/3-duty pulses (every 3rd harmonic is missing). The noise is a long LFSR clocked at 32 335/4 or 32 335/5 Hz.

keysoundwhat the ROM does

Physics simulated per sample, at 4× oversampling: a battery with internal resistance and polarisation, so the oscillator runs about 9% fast for the first ~100 ms after a press and then sags (this shows in every sound in the recording). Then the 1-bit bridged drive (±VDD) into a voice coil (R = 8 Ω, L = 0.1 mH, Bl = 0.6 T·m), a cone mass–spring–damper (0.15 g, stiffening suspension), a Helmholtz front cavity behind the grille, a rear leak through the case seams, a cone break-up mode, and absorption by the case panel. The resonances were fitted so the spectrum matches the recording within a few dB from 0.5 to 6 kHz.

The pitch steps, timings and divider ranges were measured from the recording. Which button makes which sound is my guess, read from the finger positions in the clip.
